# How to help me write a comparative literary essay

Use this guide with [WRITING_STYLE.md](WRITING_STYLE.md) when helping me plan, draft, or revise an analytical essay like my comparison of Marge Piercy's "To be of use" and W. H. Davies's "Leisure." Follow the actual assignment and my newer instructions first.

## What this guide is based on

This is a working description drawn from one essay and my teacher's feedback, not a complete profile of everything I write. The initial pasted draft is the main example of my voice. My later draft incorporates some assistant suggestions, so do not treat every polished sentence in it as independently written by me. The assistant revision is a model of improvements, not evidence of my original style.

## My voice: what to preserve

- **A personal reason for discussing the topic.** In this essay I begin as a new immigrant asking why I came to America. Use a personal opening when it fits the prompt and comes from something I actually supplied. Do not invent experiences or automatically reuse my immigration background for unrelated essays.
- **A connection between literature and everyday values.** I use the poems to think about ambition, perseverance, useful work, and enjoying life. Explain what a text means for people beyond its immediate scene.
- **Direct, understandable language.** Aim for thoughtful high-school-level English. Prefer "useful work," "daily responsibilities," and "enjoying nature" over abstract wording that adds no precision. Keep appropriate literary terms such as imagery, repetition, and tone.
- **Visible cause and effect.** I tend to explain why one idea leads to another. Keep that reasoning, but make sure each step adds something rather than restating the claim.
- **A balanced argument.** My main move is to recognize the value of one idea, then show how another text adds a limit or another need. Preserve the relationship between the ideas instead of reducing the essay to two unrelated summaries.
- **A practical ending.** I connect the reading to life today and what a fulfilling life should include. Let the ending explain why the comparison matters.

Correct grammar, spelling, awkward word choices, and excessive repetition. These are revision needs, not features to imitate. Do not make the essay sound artificially advanced or introduce fake mistakes to preserve my voice.

## The structure of this essay

The original has four paragraphs. This is a useful starting structure for a similar two-text comparison, not a required format for every assignment.

### Paragraph 1: Personal or cultural opening, then a comparative thesis

Move through these ideas:

1. Start with a real question or perspective that gives me a reason to care.
2. Connect it to a broader value or tension relevant to the prompt.
3. Introduce the first text and its central position.
4. Introduce the second text and how it complicates that position.
5. End with an arguable thesis about their relationship.

In this essay, the movement is: immigrant perspective -> American dream -> meaningful work -> need for leisure -> thesis about the limits of productivity.

A possible thesis shape is: "Although [first author] presents [value] as [benefit], [second author] complicates this idea by showing [limit or competing need]." Adapt the wording rather than repeating this sentence frame in every essay.

Keep the opening focused. Describe the American dream as a belief or ideal rather than claiming that every person actually has equal opportunities. Do not claim a poem speaks specifically about Americans unless the text or assignment supports that connection.

### Paragraph 2: Develop the first text's position

Begin with an interpretive claim that supports the thesis. Select short pieces of evidence and arrange them so the argument develops.

The useful sequence in this essay is:

- Willingness to begin work -> personal commitment.
- Continuing repeated work -> endurance beyond initial enthusiasm.
- Practical objects such as the Hopi vases -> the value of meeting other people's needs.

These examples should establish different parts of the argument. Do not use three quotations simply to repeat that the workers have perseverance. End by explaining what this view of work contributes to the larger comparison.

### Paragraph 3: Develop the second text and keep comparing

Open with a transition that names the relationship: the second author questions, qualifies, or extends the first author's position.

In this essay, the analysis moves from the repeated missed opportunities in "Leisure," to the beauty people overlook, to the idea that a busy life can still be unsatisfying. Each step deepens the cost of constant busyness.

Discuss a literary choice when it helps: how repetition builds a pattern, how an image makes a missed experience vivid, or how a judgment changes the reader's understanding of a fulfilling life. Connect this analysis back to the first text instead of waiting until the conclusion to compare them.

### Paragraph 4: Synthesize, connect, and return to the opening

Explain what reading the texts together makes clearer. Go beyond saying that both work and leisure are important.

If the prompt permits or requires outside research, connect one relevant, verified finding to this interpretation. Explain its relevance and its limits. Do not assume every future literary essay needs a study in the conclusion; a major new source may need its own body paragraph.

Finish by returning to the opening question with a more developed answer. In this essay, pursuing a better life means contributing through work while retaining the time and attention to enjoy that life.

## My teacher's feedback: evidence and commentary

The feedback reported in this chat was to avoid repeating the quote's words, weave evidence and commentary together, and address insufficient or repetitive commentary.

Apply that feedback this way:

1. **Embed short evidence inside an analytical sentence.** Let the quotation help make a claim instead of announcing it and then translating it.
2. **Interpret rather than define.** Explain what a detail reveals about motivation, responsibility, satisfaction, or another relevant idea. A dictionary definition of perseverance is not an explanation of the poem.
3. **Develop the implications.** Ask what makes the action difficult, why the author values it, what readers might normally overlook, or how it changes the comparison.
4. **Give each sentence a new job.** A useful progression is claim -> evidence -> explanation of the textual choice -> implication for the thesis. Not every quotation needs a fixed number of commentary sentences.
5. **Use qualifying language where needed.** Say "suggests" when making an interpretation. Do not present an inference as a biographical fact or something the poem explicitly states.

The feedback does not mean never repeat a word from a quotation. Closely examining a key word or a repeated phrase is useful when it explains an effect. The problem is rephrasing the same idea without advancing it.

For example, explaining that "again and again" means doing something repeatedly adds little. Explaining that necessary work continues after the excitement of starting has faded develops a claim about commitment.

Likewise, identifying boughs and stars as natural things is only identification. Explain why overlooking nearby beauty makes the speaker's criticism of a hurried life stronger.

## Evidence and accuracy

- Check quotations against the assigned text, including wording, spelling, and line breaks when relevant. Preserve wording inside quotation marks.
- Verify author names and use the author's surname naturally; avoid repeatedly writing "author Piercy" or "author Davies."
- Read the surrounding lines before explaining a quotation. The Hopi vases example emphasizes practical use; museum display alone does not prove that perseverance guarantees lasting fame.
- For research, verify the title, date, authors, and actual findings. Separate a benefit to well-being from a demonstrated improvement in performance.
- Treat historical and cultural claims as claims needing support. Do not add background facts merely because they sound plausible.
- Follow the requested citation style. Do not invent line numbers, page numbers, quotations, or assignment requirements.

## How to revise my draft

Preserve my central argument, supplied personal perspective, useful evidence, and intended organization. Replace weak analysis instead of appending stronger analysis beside it and leaving both versions in place.

Remove sentences that only repeat "this indicates," "this shows," a quotation's literal meaning, or an already established point. These phrases are not forbidden, but the sentence still needs a distinct insight.

Use transitions to explain relationships rather than mechanically adding "therefore," "finally," or "in other words." Mix sentence lengths and read the result for natural rhythm. Keep the meaning easy to follow without flattening the analysis.

When I ask exactly what to change, quote the exact current passage and provide its replacement. When I ask for a full revision, deliver a complete revised essay. Preserve my original separately if I ask for both versions.

## Final review before delivering an essay

- Does the thesis answer the actual prompt and explain the relationship between the texts?
- Does every body paragraph develop that thesis rather than summarize the text?
- Are quotations integrated, accurate, and followed by interpretation?
- Does each evidence example add a different point?
- Does the commentary explain why the evidence matters without repeating itself?
- Does the comparison remain visible throughout the essay?
- Does the conclusion develop the significance and connect back to the opening?
- Is the language clear, natural, and appropriate for me?
- Are personal details supplied by me and outside claims supported?
- Have the actual length, formatting, and citation requirements been checked?

For a requested PDF, preserve the requested section order, verify text coverage, and visually inspect the rendered pages before delivering it.
