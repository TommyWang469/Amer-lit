# O Captain! My Captain!

Name: Tommy Wang

## 1. Who is the speaker addressing?

The speaker calls out to the captain, who represents Abraham Lincoln. The ship stands for the United States, and its “fearful trip” is the Civil War. Since Lincoln was assassinated just as the Union was winning the war, “O Captain! my Captain!” sounds like both a call to Lincoln and a cry of grief. “But O heart! heart! heart!” can be read as the speaker reacting to his own pain.

## 2. Victory parade and funeral images

The bells, flying flag, bugle, and crowds along the shore suggest a victory parade. The bouquets and ribboned wreaths can welcome a hero or honor someone who has died. It’s ironic that the crowd has gathered to honor the leader who brought the country through the war, but he has died just as the ship comes home.

## 3. Tone in stanzas 1 and 2

Stanza 1 starts with relief and excitement. The “prize we sought is won,” and the people are “exulting.” The “bleeding drops of red” suddenly turn that excitement into shock. In stanza 2, the repeated “rise up” sounds desperate, and calling the death “some dream” shows how badly the speaker wants it to be unreal.

## 4. Why the speaker calls the captain “father”

“Captain” shows the speaker’s respect for his leader, while “dear father” shows how close he feels to him. The speaker even puts an arm beneath the captain’s head, like someone caring for a family member. Whitman presents Lincoln as a father figure who guided the country through the war. That makes his assassination feel like a personal loss.

## 5. Tone in stanza 3

The third stanza sounds quieter and more sorrowful. Earlier the speaker begs the captain to rise, but now he admits that his lips are “pale and still” and that he has “no pulse.” The phrase “mournful tread” makes the reader picture him walking slowly across the deck as he accepts the captain’s death.

## 6. What Whitman gives the most weight at the end

The safely anchored ship shows that the United States has made it through the Civil War. The speaker calls for the shores to rejoice and the bells to ring, giving the country room to celebrate the Union’s victory. Still, the poem ends with the speaker on the deck and the words “Fallen cold and dead.” Giving Lincoln’s death the final line makes grief the feeling that stays with the reader after the celebration.
