valuable works for the world. For example, Piercy appreciates people who persevere, collaborative
and are self-motivated. "Leisure" by W. H. Davis is an ironic poem that satirizes the life which has
no downtime for leisure is poor. Author Marge Piercy presents perseverance and committed work as
valuable; however W. D. Davis complicates this idea by warning that a life centered only on

In "To be of use", Marge Piercy communicates a key idea of American culture, which is that
perseverance creates works that are meaningful to the world. He starts the poem by appreciating
people who "jump into work headfirst". By admiring people who “jump into work headfirst”, Piercy
presents meaningful work as something people are willing to embrace. Their readiness to begin a
new work suggests that motivation comes from their own commitment rather than pressure from
others. Therefore, it indicates that these people are self-motivated. Because perseverance is used to
describe people who have willpower to do work by themselves and improve their works, this is
exactly the group of people who are appreciated by the author. The author also appreciates another
group of people "who do what has to be done, again and again", that indicates these people are able
to master their work repeatedly. Because their persistence continues even after their initial
enthusiasm has faded, perseverance is directly related to this group of people. Beginning a task with
enthusiasm is easier than continuing even after excitement has faded. Finally author Piercy
addresses how work is meaningful for the world by stating that, "Hopi vases that held corn, are put
in the museum." These ancient clay jars used by Native Americans to store and carry liquids, are
originally made to use. Because they are well made, they become art and put into the museum. Thus
this indicates that works are meaningful because if people do their work with great perseverance and
motivation, these works will be used and remembered forever like the hopi vases.

Meaningful Work and a Full Life

As a new immigrant, I often ask myself why I came to America. One answer is the American

dream: the belief that opportunity and hard work can lead to a better life. This belief encourages
people to challenge themselves and create things that benefit others. However, building a better life
also means having time to enjoy it. Marge Piercy's poem "To be of use" values people who approach
necessary work with energy and commitment. In "Leisure," W. H. Davies questions whether a busy
life is truly fulfilling when people cannot pause to appreciate the world around them. Although
Piercy presents perseverance and useful work as sources of satisfaction, Davies complicates this
idea by suggesting that achievement alone cannot make life meaningful if people lose the ability to
enjoy everyday beauty.

In "To be of use," Piercy presents perseverance as valuable because it allows people to

contribute something useful to the world. Her admiration for those who "jump into work head first"
suggests that meaningful work begins with a willingness to become fully involved. Rather than
waiting for someone else to take responsibility, these people commit their own energy to the task.
Yet starting with enthusiasm is only part of that commitment. By praising people who complete
necessary work "again and again," Piercy shifts attention from the excitement of beginning to the
patience required to continue. Repetitive tasks may bring little immediate recognition, but doing
them reliably meets needs that do not disappear when the work becomes boring or difficult. This
connection between effort and usefulness becomes clearer through the "Hopi vases that held corn."
Although these objects are now displayed in museums, Piercy emphasizes that they originally
served a practical purpose. Their value comes from more than being admired: they were made to
meet people's everyday needs. The vases suggest that ordinary work can be worthy of respect
because of what it provides for others. Through these examples, Piercy offers a way to understand
success beyond personal recognition, showing that satisfaction can come from making a dependable
contribution.

While Piercy emphasizes the satisfaction of useful work, Davies warns that constant busyness
can keep people from enjoying life. The repeated phrase "no time" makes each missed experience
feel like part of a larger pattern. People pass through a world filled with beauty, yet their attention
remains occupied by other demands. When Davies describes "streams full of stars," the image
suggests that something as ordinary as sunlight on water can reward a moment of attention.
Enjoyment does not always require an expensive vacation or a major achievement; it can come from
noticing what is already nearby. Calling a hurried existence "a poor life" therefore challenges the
idea that a full schedule necessarily means a rich life. A person may fulfill responsibilities while still
missing experiences that make those efforts worthwhile. The phrase "full of care" suggests that

worry consumes the attention people could give to their surroundings. Davies's concern is not
simply whether people take enough breaks to work efficiently afterward. His poem suggests that the
ability to appreciate beauty has value in itself. From this perspective, work becomes less fulfilling
when it leaves no room to experience the life it is meant to support.

Together, the poems suggest that the better life promised by the American dream should include

both useful contributions and time to enjoy the world. Piercy helps explain why commitment
matters, while Davies raises the question of what that commitment should ultimately make possible.
Modern research also supports taking people's need for rest seriously. In their 2022 review, "Give
me a break!," Patricia Albulescu, Irina Macsinga, and their colleagues found that short breaks
reduced fatigue and increased energy, although the overall improvement in work performance was
not statistically significant. These findings suggest that rest deserves attention because it supports
people's well-being, even when it does not clearly increase productivity. For me, this balance
changes what it means to pursue the American dream. I want my effort to lead to useful work, but I
also want enough time and attention to enjoy the life I am building.
