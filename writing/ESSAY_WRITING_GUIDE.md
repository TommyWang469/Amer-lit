# Writing a comparative literary essay

Read [WRITING_STYLE.md](WRITING_STYLE.md) for voice and light grammar editing, and [LITERARY_COMMENTARY_GUIDE.md](LITERARY_COMMENTARY_GUIDE.md) for analysis methods, sources, and the required commentary review. Apply the user's latest instructions and the actual assignment first.

## The standard to aim for

Aim for work that could earn **90/100 or higher** under the teacher's rubric. The teacher gave the original work-and-leisure essay 80/100; the user reports losing most points on commentary. The assistant revision has not been graded. Better reasoning, accurate evidence, and sustained focus are the main improvements to make. Do not promise a grade or use invented percentage weights.

Keep this chain complete throughout:

**Prompt -> central topic and thesis -> topic sentence -> evidence -> commentary proving that claim -> significance.**

Every paragraph must advance the same central argument. An interesting comment about a related theme is insufficient if it does not support that paragraph's purpose. Cut tangents and repeated reasoning. Do not infer that a poem specifically describes Americans unless the text or assignment supports that connection.

## Preferred structure

For essays like the user's two-text comparison, preserve this four-paragraph order unless the user or assignment specifies another format. Keep the organizational pattern; adapt its content to the new prompt.

### 1. Introduction: reason to care, context, texts, thesis

Begin with a focused personal or cultural question when appropriate, then connect it to the central topic. Use only personal details the user supplied. Introduce both texts and their relevant positions. End with an arguable thesis explaining their relationship, rather than merely listing two ideas.

The original essay moved from the user's immigrant perspective to the American dream, meaningful work, and the need for leisure. That is an example of a progression, not an opening to reuse for every essay. State broad cultural ideas as beliefs or ideals where appropriate rather than unsupported universal facts.

A possible thesis shape is: "Although [first author] presents [value] as [benefit], [second author] complicates this idea by showing [limit or competing need]." Use natural wording instead of mechanically repeating this frame.

### 2. First body paragraph: develop the first text's position

Start with a topic sentence that makes one interpretive claim supporting the thesis. Choose evidence with distinct jobs, explain how the author's choices create meaning, and show why that meaning proves the topic sentence.

In the user's Piercy paragraph, a useful progression is willingness to begin -> endurance through necessary tasks -> practical usefulness to other people. Do not use three quotations just to repeat that the workers show perseverance. End with what this developed view contributes to the comparison.

### 3. Second body paragraph: develop the second text in comparison

Open by explaining whether the second text questions, qualifies, extends, or challenges the first position. Develop its own claim with integrated evidence and commentary. Keep the relationship between the texts visible rather than saving the entire comparison for the conclusion.

In the Davies example, the reasoning moves from repeated missed experiences to overlooked beauty to the cost of a hurried life. Identifying imagery or repetition is not enough; explain the effect and its relevance to the paragraph's claim.

### 4. Conclusion: synthesize, connect to the world, explain why it matters

The user explicitly wants a real-world connection and an explanation of significance. Move through these steps:

1. Explain what the texts reveal together, with an answer more developed than "both are important."
2. Identify a concrete situation, choice, or concern in people's lives, communities, or society where that insight applies.
3. Explain the consequence: what people gain, lose, reconsider, or do differently because of it.
4. End with a clear statement of why the argument matters, returning to the opening question when appropriate.

Keep the connection centered on the same topic. For the work-and-leisure essay, the implication concerns how people define success and spend their time; an unrelated world issue would weaken the ending.

Outside research is optional unless required. Verify any study used, explain how its actual finding relates to the argument, and avoid presenting a major new argument for the first time in the conclusion.

## Planning and drafting

1. Read the current prompt, required texts, rubric, source pages, and teacher feedback. Identify the task verb, scope, length, citation style, source rules, and other explicit requirements. Identify missing information without inventing it; continue preparation that does not depend on it.
2. Draft a specific, defensible thesis answering every required part of the question. Give each paragraph a different part of that argument to establish.
3. Plan each section using: **paragraph claim | evidence in context | textual choice and meaning | reason it supports the claim | contribution to the thesis.** Narrow a claim or change the evidence if the reasoning does not hold.
4. Write natural paragraphs, not a fixed number of commentary sentences per quote. Let each new example extend, qualify, or develop the argument. Explain genuine tensions when useful; do not force counterarguments or device lists.
5. Follow the commentary guide's examples and review. Commentary is the main revision priority; more quotations and advanced vocabulary do not repair missing reasoning.

## Revising and checking the result

- **Argument:** Read only the thesis, topic sentences, and conclusion. They should form one focused, logical argument with distinct paragraph purposes.
- **Commentary:** Apply the eight-question review in the commentary guide to every evidence-and-commentary section. Check both how the textual detail produces meaning and why that meaning supports the actual topic sentence. Establish every central part of the paragraph's claim across its examples.
- **Repetition:** Replace weak analysis rather than appending stronger wording beside it. Keep a sentence only if it adds a detail, connection, consequence, qualification, or useful comparison.
- **Accuracy:** Verify quotation wording, context, author names, source claims, and citations. Distinguish the speaker, characters, and author. Support inferences and use appropriate qualifying language. Never invent line or page numbers.
- **Ending:** Confirm that the real-world connection follows from the body and explains significance rather than simply repeating the introduction.
- **Voice and requirements:** Apply the style guide, check the actual word count, and verify the requested format. Identify any unverified source or unmet requirement honestly.

When asked exactly what to change, quote the exact current passage and give its replacement. When asked for a full revision, provide a complete revised essay. Preserve originals separately when both versions are requested. For a PDF, check text coverage, render the pages, inspect readability and section order, and link the final file directly.
